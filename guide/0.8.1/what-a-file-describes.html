The SOLEMNE Encoding Guidelines

I What a file describes

This part says what a file describes, in the terms the rest of the guide uses: documents and their nodes, the levels of a text, codes, ids, citations and permalinks, annotations, records and relations. They're the terms of the application the files are made for, SOLEMNE's, which imports them and exports them again. A file uses them without having to name them.

Every bearer of the texts of early medieval canon law is described in the same way: a council as an edition prints it, a collection as a manuscript gives it, a collection as scholars have reconstructed it. Each keeps its own structure and its own text. How they relate, that one collection copies another or that a chapter quotes a council, is evidence, stated with its grounds by scholars and found by the application's tools; it isn't the frame a file is written in.

I.1 Documents and nodes

A file describes one document. A document is a tree of nodes, whose root stands for the document itself: the file's body. There are three kinds of node:

  • a division, which has a type and holds other nodes, never text;
  • a heading, which has a type and a text of its own;
  • a paragraph, which holds text.

A division holds headings, and either paragraphs or divisions, never both; its headings stand where the source puts them, usually first. A node knows its parent, its place among its siblings, its kind, its type, its number (a division's as a value, 49 for "XLVIIII"; the heading keeps what the source writes), its citation and its text. III The text gives the forms.

The text of a division that holds no divisions is one unit of reception: what a reader, a search or the detection of text reuse takes as one piece. Where a source sets several units side by side without headings, each is a division of the type unit. Where an encoder didn't mark the units, the application infers them, and the file says so (II.4.1 Inferred units).

Each version of a node's text has two statuses: its curation, how far it has been checked (high, medium, low, unknown), which a file may state (II.4.2 Curation); and its completeness, which the application counts from its gaps.

I.2 The levels of a text

A text has levels, after SCTA's model:

  • a work: a council's canons, a decretal, a collection;
  • its versions: a translation, a recension, one of them canonical, each with its canons, the units by which it is cited, without text of their own;
  • its witnesses: a copy in a manuscript, or an edition in a publication;
  • their transcriptions: texts with structure.

A file is the transcription of one witness: of a manuscript's copy, or of an edition. Which version is canonical is a default, not a fact. A witness keeps its own structure, which the application maps to its version's canons. A manuscript and a publication are records (V Records); a witness is "in" one of them, at its folios or its pages.

The Council of Nicaea is a work, C.NIC-0325; its canonical version takes the same code, and its third canon is C.NIC-0325_3; the first Dionysiana's translation is another version, C.NIC-0325-vDIO1; its edition by Turner is a witness, and the file that transcribes it, of the same code, its transcription. A file doesn't encode these levels: its header gives its code, and an export adds the permalinks of the levels it belongs to, in its formal title. The application's domain isn't fixed yet; the example uses example.org:

 <idno>C.NIC-0325-vDIO1</idno>
 <idno type="category">C</idno>

I.3 Codes and ids

A document's id is its code (II.1.2 The coding rules), read with its kind: a work takes the code its texts share; its canonical version the work's code, and its canons the citations that source notes use (C.NIC-0325_3); other versions a suffix (C.NIC-0325-vDIO1); a witness its transcription's code. A text of the Patrologia Latina takes .ed-PL and the volume (COLL.Burch.ed-PL140), SCTA's Gratian COLL.Grat.ed-SCTA. Codes are valid XML ids and identifiers of the Distributed Text Services: a letter first, then letters, digits, . and -; _ only before a citation. A manuscript's short code is made from its full shelfmark: Mun-BSB-4592. A record's id is its kind and a number: PERS.1234.

A file's own xml:ids are its encoder's, for the pointers inside it: fragments, declarations, records listed in the file. The import gives each node an id of its own, for good, which an export writes as the node's xml:id. Other projects' ids are kept beside the project's, as external ids (II.4.3 Other projects' ids).

I.4 Citations and permalinks

A node's citation is the path of steps from the root to it, joined by dots:

  • a chapter or a register item by its number, as a value (49); one without a number takes the number before it and a letter (11a), and a number repeated takes the next letter;
  • a section by its own number or name, or its position;
  • the other divisions by a prefix and their position: pr for a preface, cl for a closer, sub for subscriptions, r for a register, u for a unit (u1).

So the forty-ninth chapter of a collection's second book is 2.49, and a pointer to it, the document's code and the citation (COLL.Dach.ms-Brn-Burg-425_2.49; IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms). Readers see the document's own terms: "lib. 2 c. 49", "C. 16 q. 6 c. 2". A node keeps its id for good; its citation follows its place.

A permalink is https://, the application's domain, /id/, the kind and the id: a record, a document, a node, a level. A text as it stood at a time adds @ and its version (…@t3); a release is named by its name. Inside the project only the project's ids are used.

I.5 Annotations

A node's text is plain text. Everything marked in it is an annotation, anchored to a span of the text, a point, the whole node, or other annotations; spans may nest and overlap. Each kind of annotation is a layer, which says what an annotation of that kind keeps, and how it's written to TEI. Where a scribe corrected a word, the corrected reading is the text, and the change is an annotation. A note outside a division's paragraphs annotates the division; gaps, page breaks and notes between divisions anchor at the start of the next node. An annotation that can't nest where it stands is written as fragments, or by anchors and a stand-off annotation (IV.8 What no element fits).

I.6 Records, relations, observations and judgements

A record describes a person, a place, an institution, an event, a manuscript or a publication. A file points to records from its names (IV.1 Names and their records), and may list them in its standOff (V Records). A placeholder becomes a provisional record at import. Every record, text and annotation names its source: where it comes from, its id there, a link, the date it was retrieved, and its licence. A record names its own in itself (V.11 Sources), a text in its header (II.3 Publication and source).

A relation is a typed statement from one thing to another, records, nodes or relations, with a type and its reverse, a date, a certainty (certain, probable, possible), a role and a place where its type needs them, its evidence, and its author. An observation is what a witness shows: that a chapter's inscription names the council of Nicaea, that a source note gives a canon. A judgement holds of a version or a work, and names its evidence and its method: that a collection draws on the Hispana. A file may state either, signed (VI Relations); an edition's source notes are kept as observations until the project resolves them into relations. A family of texts is a view the application works out from the relations; it is never encoded.
