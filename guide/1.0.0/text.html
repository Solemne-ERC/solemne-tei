The SOLEMNE Encoding Guidelines

III The text

The text of a file is its body: the document as the source gives it, its divisions, their headings and their paragraphs. The import makes each of them a node of the document's tree (I.1 Documents and nodes) and keeps the order the file gives; an export writes them back in the same form, each with the id the node got at import. This part says how the text is divided and how it is transcribed; IV What is marked in the text says what may be marked inside it.

A file needs no division at all: a body holding paragraphs is a document of one node's text, and an empty body passes the checks. Most files divide their text, and divisions, their headings and page breaks are common; units, registers and the transcription's finer marks are full.

Inside the body stand only the elements of the text's structure and of the annotation layers (IV What is marked in the text). An element the schema doesn't take from TEI at all fails the RelaxNG; one that the schema takes for the header or the records, such as orgName, fails the Schematron when it stands in the text.

Breaks element-outside-the-schema:

<p>Placuit ut <emph>episcopi</emph> maneant.</p>
<!-- breaks the rule -->

Breaks text-elements:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi <orgName>ecclesiae</orgName> maneant.</p>

III.1 Divisions

A division (div) holds headings, then either paragraphs or divisions, never both. Its headings stand where the source puts them, usually first. Page breaks, notes and gaps may stand between headings, paragraphs and divisions (III.9 What stands between). The body follows the same rule: it holds headings with either paragraphs or divisions.

Every division has a type, from a closed list, so that a misspelled type fails the RelaxNG:

  • section: a book, a part, or one text inside a document, such as a council in a collection;
  • chapter: a numbered or titled unit, as the source divides it; chapters may hold chapters;
  • unit: a subdivision without a heading of its own, where several stand side by side (III.4 Units);
  • preface, closer and subscriptions: what opens a text, what closes it (a closing rubric, a letter's farewell and date), and its signatories (III.6 Prefaces, closers and subscriptions);
  • register and registerItem: a list of chapter titles and its entries (III.5 Registers);
  • part, distinctio, causa, quaestio, capitulum and dictum: Gratian's levels (III.7 Gratian's levels).

Divisions follow the source: an edition's chapters as its editor numbers them, a manuscript's as its rubrics and initials mark them. Where the source marks no division, the text stays in paragraphs; the guide doesn't ask for divisions the source doesn't have. The Council of Mainz of 813, in Werminghoff's edition, is a series of chapters, each with a rubric and a number:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="rubric">
  <pb n="261"/>De fide catholica firmiter retinenda.</head>
 <head type="number">Cap.I</head>
 <p>Initium enim actionis nostrae de fide esse decrevimus, quae bonorum est omnium fundamentum, …</p>
</div>
<div type="chapter" n="2">
 <head type="rubric">De spe habenda in Deum.</head>
 <head type="number">Cap.II</head>
 <p>Spes vero in Deum firmiter a cunctis Christianis tenenda est. …</p>
</div>

The names and quotations that the file marks in these chapters are left out here; IV What is marked in the text shows them. A division without its type, or with a type not in the list, fails the RelaxNG, and so does a division that holds paragraphs and divisions side by side:

Breaks division-type-missing:

<div n="1">
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

Breaks division-type-misspelled:

<div type="chpater" n="1">
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

Breaks division-holds-paragraphs-and-divisions:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>

The import makes each division a node of the kind division, with its type; a division's paragraphs, where it holds no divisions, are one unit of reception (III.4 Units).

III.2 Headings

A heading (head) is a node of its own, with its own text, and has a type:

  • number: the division's number as the source writes it, with the word that goes with it ("Cap. II.", "CAPUT V.", "XLVIIII.");
  • rubric: what the text is about ("De spe habenda in Deum.");
  • inscription: where the text comes from ("Ex eadem Epistola."). A heading that begins with its source is an inscription, even when it goes on with its subject.

A division may have several headings, of one type or of several, in the order the source gives them. The Collectio Dacheriana, in d'Achery's edition, gives a chapter's number, its rubric and its inscription:

<div type="chapter" n="5">
 <pb n="519"/>
 <head type="number">CAPUT V.</head>
 <head type="rubric">Quod ad militiam saecularem post poenitentiam redire non liceat.</head>
 <head type="inscription">Ex eadem Epistola.</head>
 <p>Contrarium est omnino ecclesiasticis regulis post poenitentiae actionem redire ad militiam saecularem, cum
   Apostolus dicat: Nemo militans Deo implicat se negotiis saecularibus. …</p>
</div>

The Council of Aachen of 816 heads a chapter with its source, then its subject: one inscription.

<div type="chapter" n="49">
 <head type="number">XLVIIII.</head>
 <head type="inscription">In Antioceno concilio de peregrinorum susceptione, t. VII.</head>
 <p>Nullus peregrinorum sine pacificis, id est commendaticiis, suscipiatur epistolis.</p>
</div>

A heading may head the whole text: the body holds it before the text's divisions, as a division holds its headings, so that the chapters below are cited by their own numbers (9, not 1.9). Childebert II's decree, in Boretius's edition, opens with its title, then its preface and its chapters:

<body>
 <head type="rubric">Incipit decretio Childeberti regis.</head>
 <div type="preface">
  <p>Childebertus, rex Francorum, vir inluster. Cum in Dei nomine nos omnes Kalendas Martias de quascumque
     condiciones una cum nostris optimatibus pertractavimus, ad unumquemque noticia volumus pervenire.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="chapter" n="1">
  <head type="number">1.</head>
  <p>Ita, Deo propitiante, Antonaco Kalendas Marcias anno vicesimo regni nostri convenit, ut nepotes ex filio
     vel ex filia ad aviaticas res cum avunculos vel amitas sic venirent, tamquam si pater aut mater vivi
     fuissent. …</p>
 </div>
</body>

A heading without a type, or with one not in the list, fails the RelaxNG:

Breaks heading-type-missing:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head>De episcopis.</head>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

Breaks heading-type-misspelled:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="inscripto">De episcopis.</head>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

An inscription is also a claim about the text's source: the application reads it as an attribution, the source the text claims (IV.3 Statements, references, quotations, formulae).

III.3 Numbers

A numbered division carries its number as a value in n: digits, and a letter after them where needed. The number heading keeps the number as the source writes it; n gives what it counts, so that citations can use it: 49 for "XLVIIII.", 5 for "CAPUT V.". A chapter without a number after a numbered one takes that number and a letter (11a after 11); a number the source repeats takes the next letter. A division with a number heading has n (a Schematron error otherwise), unless it's cited by its prefix and position, a preface, a closer, subscriptions or a register, whose number heading keeps the source's words alone (III.6 Prefaces, closers and subscriptions); and n holds nothing but the value (a RelaxNG error):

Breaks number-heading-n:

<div type="chapter">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

Breaks division-number-not-a-value:

<div type="chapter" n="I">
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
</div>

The import keeps the value as the node's number, and the citation of a chapter or a register item is made from it (I.4 Citations and permalinks). A division without a number heading may still have n, where the numbering of the file gives one. One kind of value isn't a number: a book of the Bible, a section, has its book's code (III.8 The Bible).

III.4 Units

A unit (unit) is a subdivision without a heading of its own, written only where several stand side by side: a norm and the authority statements that follow it, each signatory among the subscriptions, the paragraphs of a preface that are read one by one, a law and the interpretatio that follows it in the Breviary. A division whose text is one unit holds its paragraphs itself: a unit that stands alone is an error, and so is a unit that holds headings or divisions.

The preface of the Council of Mainz of 813 is three units, the greeting, the account of the council, and its decision:

<div type="preface">
 <div type="unit">
  <p>
   <pb n="259"/>In nomine patris et filii et Spiritus sancti. Gloriosissimo et christianissimo imperatori
     Karolo Augusto, verae religionis rectori ac defensori sanctae Dei eclesiae, una cum prole sua eiusque
     fidelibus vita et salus, honor et benedictio cum victoria sine fine mansura.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Almifice reverentiae vestrae patefacimus nos humillimi famuli ac missi vestri, … modis quibus poterant
     iusticias terminantes.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>In his itaque omnibus primo decrevimus unicuique personae vel sexui congruum honorem impendere … in
     omnes aeternitates saeculi, amen.</p>
 </div>
</div>

Breaks unit-alone:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
 </div>
</div>

Breaks unit-holds-text:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <head type="rubric">De episcopis.</head>
 <div type="unit">
  <head type="rubric">De eodem.</head>
  <p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Et clerici.</p>
 </div>
</div>

The Codex Theodosianus, in The Latin Library's text, gives the Breviary's interpretatio after many laws: the law's text and the interpretatio are two units of the law. That the second is a later commentary on the first is for a relation or an annotation to say, not for the division's type:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">CTh.1.1.1 [=brev.1.1.1]</head>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Imp. constantinus a. ad lusitanos. si qua posthac edicta sive constitutiones sine die et consule fuerint
     deprehensa, auctoritate careant. dat. vii. kal. aug. sabariae, probiano et iuliano vv. cc. coss.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>interpretatio. quaecumque leges sine die et consule fuerint prolatae, non valeant</p>
 </div>
</div>

The text of a division that holds no divisions, a unit or a chapter of paragraphs, is one unit of reception: what a reader, a search or the detection of text reuse takes as one piece. Where the encoder didn't mark the units, so that a chapter's paragraphs may be several units, the header says so, for the whole file or for some divisions (II.4.1 Inferred units).

III.5 Registers

A register (register) is a list of a text's chapter titles; each entry is a registerItem, with the number and the title the register gives, and stands in a register (a Schematron error otherwise). A file that is only a register holds one register division in its body. The register of the Collectio 400 capitulorum in a Munich manuscript begins:

<div type="register">
 <div type="registerItem" n="1">
  <head type="number">
   <pb n="158ra"/>I</head>
  <head type="rubric">De confirmatione caritatis dei</head>
 </div>
 <div type="registerItem" n="2">
  <head type="number">II</head>
  <head type="rubric">De amore confirmata proximi per deum</head>
 </div>
 <div type="registerItem" n="3">
  <head type="number">III</head>
  <head type="rubric">De deo quod non est personarum acceptor</head>
 </div>
</div>

Breaks register-item-in-register:

<div type="registerItem" n="2">
 <head type="number">II.</head>
</div>

Which chapter a register item lists is a relation (VI Relations), not a pointer in the text.

III.6 Prefaces, closers and subscriptions

What opens a text before its chapters is a preface (above, III.4 Units); what closes it, a closing rubric or a letter's farewell and date, a closer; its signatories, subscriptions, each signatory a unit. The Council of Aschheim, 756, ends with a closing rubric, and the Council of Soissons, 744, with its signatories:

<div type="closer">
 <head type="rubric">FINIT.</head>
</div>
<div type="subscriptions">
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Signum inluster vir Pippino, maiorum domius.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Signum Radobodo.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="unit">
  <p>Signum Ariberto.</p>
 </div>
</div>

A law of a Roman code opens with its principium, before its numbered paragraphs, which the editions cite as "pr.": it is the law's preface, cited pr1. Its id line, where the source gives one, is its number heading, without n: a preface, a closer, subscriptions and a register are cited by their prefix and position, not by a number (I.4 Citations and permalinks). A law of the Codex Theodosianus, in The Latin Library's text, its principium LR.CTh-0438_16.2.47.pr1 and its paragraph LR.CTh-0438_16.2.47.1:

<div type="chapter" n="47">
 <div type="preface">
  <head type="number">CTh.16.2.47pr.</head>
  <p>Idem a. et caes. basso comiti rerum privatarum. privilegia ecclesiarum omnium, quae seaculo nostro
     tyrannus inviderat, prona devotione revocamus, … serventur.</p>
 </div>
 <div type="chapter" n="1">
  <head type="number">CTh.16.2.47.1</head>
  <p>Clericos etiam, quos indiscretim ad saeculares iudices debere deduci infaustus praesumptor edixerat,
     episcopali audientiae reservamus. …</p>

III.7 Gratian's levels

Gratian's Decretum has levels of its own, which the citations of its readers use ("C. 16 q. 6 c. 2"): a part, a distinctio, a causa and a quaestio are kinds of section; a capitulum (a canon) and a dictum (Gratian's own words between the canons) are kinds of chapter. They're types like the others, each with its number in n. The skeleton of a question of a causa, its text left out:

<div type="part" n="2">
 <div type="causa" n="16">
  <div type="quaestio" n="6">
   <div type="dictum" n="1">
    <p>…</p>
   </div>
   <div type="capitulum" n="2">
    <p>…</p>
   </div>
  </div>
 </div>
</div>

III.8 The Bible

The Bible is one file, the Vulgate (BIB.Vulg, of the category BIB), divided by book, chapter and verse, so that the pointers to it (IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms) name its nodes without a table between them. A book is a section, its code in n (MAT, one of the 73 the pointers use) and its name as a rubric; a chapter is a chapter, numbered; a verse is a chapter inside it, numbered, its text a paragraph, since a chapter is a numbered unit as the source divides it and is cited by its number. So MAT_18_19 names the verse cited MAT.18.19, JOH_1 the chapter JOH.1, and a range, PHP_3_17-20, its run of verses. The Psalms keep the Vulgate's numbers, and a psalm's title stays in its first verse, as the Clementine text prints it. A book's prologue, which the Clementine text prints before the first verse, as Sirach's and Lamentations', is a preface of its book, before its first chapter, cited pr1 (SIR.pr1), its heading as the text gives it. Matthew 18:19–20, made up from the Vulgate's words:

  </div>

III.9 What stands between

Page breaks, notes and gaps may stand between headings, paragraphs and divisions, where the source has them. The application anchors each at the start of the next node: a page break before a chapter belongs to the chapter's first heading. The edition of the Roman council of 826 leaves out several pages after a heading, which the file says by a gap and a note:

<div type="section">
 <head type="rubric">B. CANONES CONCILII ROMANI.</head>
 <gap reason="editorial"/>
 <note type="editorial">Several pages of the edition are left out here: the introduction and the register of
   the council.</note>
 <div type="chapter" n="1">
  <head type="number">
   <pb n="566"/>I.</head>
  <head type="rubric">Ut episcopus bono adprobatus opere ordinetur.</head>
  <p>Beatissimi Pauli discretione atque ammonitione episcopum consecrari omnino oportet desiderabilem boni
     operis, … quae <pb n="567"/>secuntur, …</p>
 </div>
</div>

III.10 The transcription

These rules strike a balance between the practices of two projects, Capitularia and Carolingian Canon Law: precise where the text is concerned, plain where its layout is.

III.10.1 Editions and manuscripts

The transcription gives the text of the source, whatever it is, keeping its structure, its spelling and its punctuation, with the exceptions below. From an edition, the text, its divisions, spelling and punctuation are the editor's, obvious misprints aside. The critical apparatus isn't encoded; where it gives something the guide needs, such as a later hand's change, it may be used. From a manuscript, the spelling and punctuation are the scribe's, obvious slips aside, and the divisions follow the rubrication.

Layout comes second: what is written, and how the text is organised, rather than where it stands on the page. So the layout is encoded only in page breaks, in where a change was made (above the line, in the margin), and in what can't be read.

A charter's signs, which an edition gives in words, a chrismon ("C."), a monogram ("(Monogramma.)"), a seal ("Cum sigillo …"), stay text where the edition puts them: they have no mark of their own.

III.10.2 Page breaks

A page break (pb) stands where the page begins, and gives the page in n, which is required: an edition's page (255), a manuscript's folio with r or v (120r), and a column by a letter after it (356vb). There's no column break: a page break carries its column. A page break may point to its image, the canvas of a IIIF manifest, in facs; in an edition that gives the pages of several manuscripts, each page break names its manuscript in edRef (VIII Manuscripts). A Munich manuscript of the Collectio 400 capitulorum turns to folio 177r inside a quotation:

<p>… Non duplicatio <pb n="177r"/>coniugii sed profectus est honestatis.</p>

Breaks page-break-without-number:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi<pb/>
<!-- breaks the rule --> in ciuitatibus suis maneant.</p>

III.10.3 Another edition's pages and numbering

A text is often cited by another edition's pages or numbering than the one the file follows: Pseudo-Isidore by Hinschius's pages and chapters, the Quadripartitus by Kerff's numbers, Gregory's letters by the Maurists'. That edition is a witness of the same text, listed in the source description's listWit, a witness pointing to its publication (V.7 Witnesses). Its pages are page breaks that name it in edRef, as an edition gives a manuscript's folios; a page break without edRef is the file's own edition's page.

Its numbering is a milestone (milestone) where its unit begins: unit says what it numbers (book, chapter, canon, letter, section, a warning outside them), n gives the number as the edition gives it, and edRef names the witness. Another edition's chapters needn't begin where the file's do, so a milestone stands where its unit begins: between divisions and paragraphs, or inside a paragraph or a heading. A page is a page break, never a milestone. A made-up chapter of Pseudo-Isidore, in an edition that gives Hinschius's pages and chapters, his chapter 5 beginning inside the file's chapter 4:

<listWit>
 <witness xml:id="wit-hinschius">
  <bibl>
   <ptr target="#new_bibl_hinschius_1863"/>
  </bibl>
 </witness>
</listWit>
<div type="chapter" n="4">
 <p>
  <pb n="17"/>Exemplum verborum, quae in hoc capitulo leguntur, <pb edRef="#wit-hinschius" n="131"/>et
   alia. <milestone unit="chapter" n="5"
   edRef="#wit-hinschius"/>Aliud exemplum, quod sequitur.</p>
</div>

A letter of Gregory's Register, cited by the Maurists' numbers in the Patrologia Latina and in older works, holds the Maurists' number in a milestone typed letter where its text begins. Clavis canonum's keys stay on the divisions, in sameAs (II.4.3 Other projects' ids); a Clavis location that differs from the file's numbering, such as RR1.97 for Regino's book 1, chapter 97, is a milestone of the edition the Clavis follows.

Pointers keep citing the file's own numbering: a citation by another edition's is resolved to the file's divisions before it is written (IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms). The import keeps each milestone as an annotation of its own layer, another numbering: the edition, the unit and the number, for search and citation, never as the file's own divisions (I.5 Annotations).

III.10.4 Abbreviations, spelling, punctuation

Abbreviations are expanded silently. One whose expansion is doubtful may be given with its expansions, as a choice of abbr and expan; the corpus has none so far, so the example is made up:

<p>… secundum <choice>
  <abbr>rem</abbr>
  <expan>rationem</expan>
 </choice> …</p>

The spelling is the source's: "ę" for an e caudata, the diphthongs ‘ae’ and ‘oe’ as written, and capitals where the manuscript has them. The transcription isn't diplomatic: no special characters for the letters' forms. A manuscript's punctuation may be simplified: a point as ‘.’, a punctus elevatus and other double signs as ‘;’, a comma as ‘,’. An edition's punctuation is kept. What the file normalises, it says once in the header (II.4 How the text was encoded).

III.10.5 Gaps and lacunae

A gap (gap) stands where the text is missing, and may say why, in reason, from a closed list; without a reason, the reason is unknown:

  • lacuna: the witness lacks the text: lost leaves, or a lacuna in the edition it comes from;
  • damage: the support is damaged where the text stood;
  • illegible: the text can't be read (unclear is for what can be half read);
  • reproduction: the text can't be read in the images or the film used;
  • deleted: struck out, and not readable;
  • sampling: not transcribed, by the encoder's choice, as when a text is given by its incipit and explicit;
  • editorial: left out for another reason, which a note gives.

Its extent, where known, is a quantity with its unit (leaves, lines, words, chars), which go together, or extent in words. A text given by its incipit and explicit is one paragraph, with a gap for what is left out between them:

<p>Incipit … <gap reason="sampling"
  extent="the rest of the letter"/> … explicit.</p>

Breaks gap-reason-unknown:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant. <gap reason="missing"/>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
</p>

Breaks gap-unit-unknown:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant. <gap reason="lacuna" quantity="2"
  unit="folios"/>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
</p>

Breaks gap-quantity-unit:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant. <gap reason="lacuna" quantity="2"/>
</p>

The application counts a text's completeness from its gaps: it is never stated.

Element gapchange

III.10.6 Illegible text and errors

What can be half read is unclear; what can't be read at all is a gap with the reason illegible, or damage, or reproduction:

<head type="rubric">De ablutione <unclear>infantis</unclear>.</head>

An error of the source is kept, and may be marked by sic, alone or in a choice with its correction, corr. For the rare cases where the editor of the file adds what the source lacks, the addition is supplied. Both are editorial notes on the text (IV.5 Notes: sources, parallels, reception, editorial remarks).

III.10.7 Scribal changes and their hands

A scribe's or a later reader's change is encoded where it stands: an addition by add, with place (above, below, margin, …), a deletion by del, and a correction, the one replacing the other, by subst holding both. Where a scribe corrected a word, the corrected reading is the text, and the application keeps the other reading with the change. A change may name the hand that made it, in hand, pointing to the hand described in the manuscript's description (VIII Manuscripts). In the Munich manuscript of the Collectio 400 capitulorum, a letter is added above the line, and a word replaced:

<quote source="LEO_ep.167_6">Ancillam a thoro abi<add place="above">g</add>ere. …</quote>
<head type="rubric">Non coire cum omni <subst>
  <del>pecode</del>
  <add>masculo</add>
 </subst>
</head>

A correction whose added and deleted parts can't be told apart is a seg typed corr. Line breaks (lb) are kept where a file has them, and the import drops them.
