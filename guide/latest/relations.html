The SOLEMNE Encoding Guidelines

VI Relations

A relation is a typed statement from one thing to another: a chapter is a text of a council's canon, a manuscript was owned by an abbey, a hand was a scribe's, a man was bishop of a see. It joins records, nodes of texts, or other relations, and may carry a date, a certainty, a role and a place, its evidence, its author, and, for a judgement, its method. Facts that belong to one record alone are its fields (V Records); what joins two things, may be uncertain or disputed, or needs evidence, is a relation.

A file writes its relations in the standOff's listRelation, one relation each. The import reads them as the encoder's statements, beside the observations it makes itself from the source notes (IV.5 Notes: sources, parallels, reception, attributions, editorial remarks) and the relations it makes from a description's history (VIII Manuscripts). Relations are full.

VI.1 The form of a relation

  • its type: name, the type's name (isTextOf), and, in exports, ref, the type's permalink;
  • its ends: active, what it is from, and passive, what it is to; for a symmetric type, mutual, both ends, once. An end is an id in the file (#hand-A), a placeholder, a permalink, or the code of a document or a canon, as source names them (IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms);
  • observation or judgement: type, by where the relation starts: observation from a transcription's node, for what a copy shows; judgement from a passage, a version or a work, for what holds of the text, naming its evidence; none from a record (a person, a place, an institution, a hand, a group), which is neither. The application reads the type so, writes it so in its exports, and reports a file's type that disagrees;
  • its certainty: cert, high, medium or low, for certain, probable and possible; none means certain. A disagreement is two relations, not a fourth value;
  • its author: resp, pointing to the person who states it, or, in an export, to an account with no person record (X.5 Statuses and history);
  • its date, where the type has one (an owner's years, an office's), in the relation's own attributes, in the one form (VII Dates);
  • its evidence: the passages it rests on, by pointers in source, and the publications, with their pages, in its description.

TEI 4.12 lets a relation hold one desc, and nothing else. What the type needs beyond its ends goes there, each in its element:

  • a role in what the relation joins (an editor's, a participant's at a council), by roleName;
  • where a role was held (a bishop's see), by placeName or orgName with ref;
  • a name as a publication prints it, for editor, by persName;
  • a judgement's method ("in all witnesses", "in the canonical witness", "the editor's text", "the register"), by term typed method;
  • what a text came through, for derivedFrom, by ptr typed via, pointing to the work, version or transcription;
  • a publication as evidence, by a bibl holding a ptr to it and its pages in citedRange.

VI.2 The types

Each type says what it joins, and has a reverse: "a version of" reads back as "has as a version"; a symmetric type reads the same both ways, and joins its two ends by mutual. The six types of the texts' levels and units are fixed; the others are a vocabulary of the application, which grows as the project needs. The guide holds the types to start with, each named by a plain key, and, where it has one, the term of SAWS, the Sharing Ancient Wisdoms ontology, that the application maps it to ("nearest" where SAWS's says a little more or less); an export writes it beside the type (X.6 The vocabularies). A name outside them is reported, so that a slip is seen and a new type is added to the vocabulary on purpose.

VI.3 The levels' relations

The four relations between the levels, versionOf, witnessOf, transcriptionOf and in, are the ones an imported file needs least: the import makes them from the header. A file's text is a transcription of its witness; the witness is in the manuscript or the publication its source description gives (V.7 Witnesses), at its folios or pages, and is a witness of the work its code names. An encoder writes them only to say more: that a version listed in the file is a version of a work, or that a witness is of a version and not only of its work. An export writes the levels' ids in the formal title (II.1 The title statement), and their relations in its standOff.

Which work a version belongs to is a judgement, as which version a witness carries is: versionOf, as witnessOf, takes a certainty and its evidence, and the import keeps both. A made-up version, probably of Nicaea's canons, after an edition:

<listRelation>
 <relation name="versionOf"
  type="judgement" cert="medium"
  active="#new_version_nicaea_dionysius_ii" passive="C.NIC-0325">
  <desc>after <bibl>
    <ptr target="#new_bibl_maassen_1870"/>
    <citedRange unit="page" from="12"
     to="13">12–13</citedRange>
   </bibl>
  </desc>
 </relation>
</listRelation>

isTextOf joins a node of a witness to its version's canon: it is how a witness's own structure is mapped to the canons (I.2 The levels of a text). The import makes it as an observation from each source note that names a canon; an encoder writes it where no note does.

Its narrower types say more about how one unit's text is another's: isVerbatimOf, isVariantOf, isShorterVersionOf and isLongerVersionOf, after SAWS; sameAs, correctionOf, closeRenderingOf, looseRenderingOf and paraphraseOf. Each joins two units, as isTextOf does, and a search for isTextOf finds them too.

A reception note's codes read the other way: each names a later text that took the passage over (IV.5 Notes: sources, parallels, reception, attributions, editorial remarks). The import keeps the note's words and codes; the relation it makes of each code, from the later text to the passage, and where that relation ends when the passage runs over several nodes, are still to be settled with the application.
