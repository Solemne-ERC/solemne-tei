The SOLEMNE Encoding Guidelines

IV What is marked in the text

Inside its paragraphs and headings, a file may mark what the text says: the names of persons, places and events, the works it names, the statements it makes with their references and quotations, its formulae, the sources and parallels an edition gives, editorial remarks, dates and other languages, beside the scribal changes, gaps and unclear words of III.9 The transcription. In the application a node's text is plain text, and each mark is an annotation of a layer, anchored to a span of the text or a point in it (I.5 Annotations). The import splits the marked text into its plain text and its annotations; an export writes each annotation back in the same form, inline where it nests in the others, and in the standOff where it doesn't (X Exports).

Nothing in this part is required. Names of persons are common, since most files mark them; the rest is full: a file marks as much as its encoder claims.

IV.1 Names and their records

A person's, a place's or an event's name is marked by persName, placeName or eventName, around the words of the text, and points to its record in ref, in one of three forms:

  • a placeholder: #new_, the kind of record, _, and a name of the encoder's own (#new_person_Arno), the same for every name of that record in the file;
  • an id in the file (#PERS.1234), where the record is listed in the file's standOff (V Records);
  • a permalink of the application (https://…/id/person/PERS.1234).

The import makes a provisional record of each placeholder, one for all the names that share it, for the project to identify; it uses a permalink as given, without matching: a record merged since forwards to the record that replaced it, and a permalink that leads nowhere is reported. An export writes permalinks. In the preface of the Council of Mainz of 813, the king's envoys name themselves and the city:

<p>Almifice reverentiae vestrae patefacimus nos humillimi famuli ac missi vestri, <persName ref="#new_person_Hildibaldus">Hildibaldus</persName> scilicet sacri palatii archiepiscopus, <persName ref="#new_person_rihholfus">Rihholfus</persName> et <persName ref="#new_person_Arno">Arno</persName>
archiepiscopi seu <persName ref="#new_person_Bernhard">Bernharius</persName>, una cum reliquis coepiscopis
atque abbatibus et caetero clero, quia venimus secundum iussionem vestram in civitatem <placeName ref="#new_place_Mainz">Mogontiam</placeName> …</p>

An event is named the same way; the council names itself:

<p>… Placuit itaque <eventName ref="#new_event_mainz">sancto concilio,</eventName> ut ita discernantur, sicut
in regula clericorum dictum est. …</p>

A pointer to a record has one of the three forms, or fails the RelaxNG; a placeholder without its # is the commonest slip:

Breaks record-pointer-form:

<p>Placuit <persName ref="new_person_Paul">Paulo</persName>
<!-- breaks the rule --> ut episcopi maneant.</p>

A record of a kind no name element fits, a diocese or a religious order, is marked by rs (IV.8 What no element fits).

IV.2 Works named

A work the text names, a book of the Bible, a Father's treatise, a council's canons, is marked by bibl, and points to the work's record in ref, in the forms of the names' records. The names inside it stay names. The council of 813 sets its bishops to read the Gospel and Gregory's pastoral book, and quotes Jerome's letter to Nepotian:

<p>… legentes atque tractantes <bibl ref="#new_bibl_gospels">sanctum evangelium</bibl> necnon epistolas et
actus apostolorum, canones quoque ac diversa sanctorum patrum opuscula <bibl ref="#new_bibl_pastoralcare">pastoralemque librum Gregorii</bibl> cum caeteris sacris dogmatibus …</p>
<cit>
 <ref>Tamen <persName ref="#new_person_jerome">Hieronimus</persName> in <bibl ref="#new_bibl_nepotianum">epistola ad <persName ref="#new_person_Nepotianum">Nepotianum</persName>
  </bibl>
   dicit:</ref>
 <quote>Omnium Christianorum domos quasi proprias amare debemus, …</quote>
</cit>
Element biblchange

IV.3 Statements, references, quotations, formulae

A statement cites an authority: it is a cit holding its reference, ref, the words that name the authority, and its quotation, quote, the words quoted. Whether the quotation is exact, or rightly attributed, doesn't change the form: the statements attributed to an authority are worth finding even where the attribution is wrong. A quotation names its source in source (IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms); the words that introduce an authority by a set phrase ("ut lex dicit", "unde psalmista commemorat") are a formula, a seg typed formula, marked and not yet identified. Chapter II of the Council of Mainz, 813, is a chain of statements:

<div type="chapter" n="2">
 <head type="rubric">De spe habenda in <persName ref="#new_person_god">Deum</persName>.</head>
 <head type="number">Cap.II</head>
 <p>Spes vero in <persName ref="#new_person_god">Deum</persName> firmiter a cunctis Christianis tenenda est.
 <cit>
   <ref>
    <seg type="formula">Unde psalmista commemorat:</seg>
   </ref>
   <quote source="PSA_61_9">Sperate in
       eo omnis congregatio populi et reliqua</quote>
  </cit>, <cit>
   <ref>
    <seg type="formula">et
         iterum:</seg>
   </ref>
   <quote source="PSA_36_3">Spera in <persName ref="#new_person_god">Domino</persName>
       et fac bonitatem</quote>
  </cit>, … <cit>
   <ref>
    <seg type="formula">et apostolus:</seg>
   </ref>
   <quote source="ROM_8_24">Spe enim salvi facti sumus.</quote>
  </cit>
 </p>
</div>

The application reads a statement as three annotations: the reference, the quotation with its source, and the attribution, the source the reference claims. A reference whose claim sits in a heading rather than in the text's words is typed from_paratext, the only type a reference in the text takes; a link to a manuscript's images is typed iiif (VIII Manuscripts). A quotation has no type: its source goes in source. A formula, and every other seg, has a type from its closed list.

Breaks reference-type-unknown:

<cit>
 <ref type="paratext">Dominus ait:</ref>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
 <quote source="MAT_5_3">Beati pauperes</quote>
</cit>

Breaks quotation-typed:

<cit>
 <ref>Apostolus ait:</ref>
 <quote type="ROM_12_5">unum corpus sumus</quote>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
</cit>

Breaks seg-type-missing:

<p>
 <seg>Si quis</seg>
<!-- breaks the rule --> episcopus migrauerit, deponatur.
</p>

Breaks seg-type-misspelled:

<p>
 <seg type="fomula">Si quis</seg>
<!-- breaks the rule --> episcopus migrauerit, deponatur.
</p>

IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms

A quotation's source, and a source or reception note's, hold one pointer or more, separated by spaces, each in one of these forms:

  • a document's code (I.3 Codes and ids), of any category, alone (C.NIC-0325) or with a citation after an underscore, the canon or chapter of that document (C.NIC-0325_3, D.LEO-0447-414_11, COLL.Dach.ms-Brn-Burg-425_2.49); an underscore stands only there;
  • a Bible reference: a book's code, the chapter, and the verse, each range with a hyphen (MAT_18_19, PHP_3_17-20, JOH_1); the Psalms by the Vulgate's numbers;
  • a work by its siglum in Gryson's Répertoire général des auteurs ecclésiastiques latins: the author, the work and the place cited (IS_ety_1.2.3, LEO_ep.167_6);
  • an SCTA resource, by its address (http://scta.info/resource/…).

The first three may begin with cf_ for a loose reference: a parallel or an echo rather than a quotation. The Collectio Dacheriana, in d'Achery's edition, echoes Ezekiel:

<p>… uenia peccatorum fideliter præsumatur ab illo, qui <quote source="cf_EZE_18_23">non uult mortem peccatoris,
   sed ut conuertatur</quote> a perditione poenitendo, et saluatus miseratione Domini uiuat. …</p>

A code's prefix is its category's, as the coding rules make it (II.1.2 The coding rules): COLL for both kinds of collection, the category itself for the others, LR, CPT and CHA among them. A file of unknown category, whose code may take any prefix, is reached by a pointer only under one of these. The pattern also takes W, which no category makes: it is kept, and no file uses it yet. A text that takes over a law of the Codex Theodosianus points to it by the law and its paragraph; the example is made up:

<p>Clericos etiam … episcopali audientiae reservamus.<note type="source"
  source="LR.CTh-0438_16.2.47.1">Cod.
   Theod. 16, 2, 47, 1</note>
</p>

The 73 books of the Bible have one code each. Their names are the Nova Vulgata's: the older Vulgate's I to IV Regum are 1SA, 2SA, 1KI and 2KI. solemne-tei lists prints the same table.

The RelaxNG checks each pointer's form: an older code, an underscore inside a code, a book that isn't among the 73 or a verse that isn't a number fails. It doesn't check that the document or the verse exists; the import resolves each pointer into a relation to a canon, and reports what it can't resolve.

IV.5 Notes: sources, parallels, reception, editorial remarks

A note in the text has a type, its layer:

  • source: where a passage comes from, as an edition says it: its words, and the codes of its sources in source;
  • parallel: a parallel elsewhere, an edition's "Cf." note;
  • reception: the later texts that took the passage over, as an edition's tier of later collections gives them ("Burchard I, 135; Ivo, Decr. V, 234"): the reverse of a source, the codes of the later texts in source;
  • editorial: a remark of the editor of the file, about the text or the transcription.

A note stands where its source puts it: at a point of a paragraph, or between paragraphs and divisions, where it annotates the division. The Council of Mainz, 813, in its chapter on the sacraments, cites Leo's decretal, which its editor identifies in a note, and the editor notes a parallel in a later council:

<div type="chapter" n="4">
 <head type="rubric">De sacramentis ecclesiae.</head>
 <head type="number">IIII</head>
 <p>Sacramenta itaque baptismatis volumus, … <cit>
   <ref>sicut in decretis <persName ref="#new_person_leo">Leonis papae</persName> sub duobus continetur capitulis, in capitulo
       XL:</ref>
   <quote source="D.LEO-0447-414_11">Haec duo tempora, id est Pascha et Pentecosten, ad
       baptizandum a Romano pontifice legitima praefixa sunt<note type="source">Leonis decreta c. 11
         (Jaffe-K. n. 414).</note>
   </quote>
  </cit>, …</p>
 <note type="parallel">Cf. concil. Mogunt. 847 c. 3; l. I. p. 176.</note>
</div>

Where an edition's source note is only codes, they go in source, in the pointers' forms, and the note holds no words; codes left in a note's words are reported. A note holds words, or its codes, or both.

<p>… legitima praefixa sunt<note type="source"
  source="D.LEO-0447-414_11"/> …</p>

Breaks source-note-codes:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi maneant.<note type="source">C.NIC-0325_15</note>
</p>

Breaks note-has-content:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi maneant.<note type="editorial"/>
</p>

Breaks note-type-misspelled:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi maneant.<note type="souce" source="C.NIC-0325_3"/>
<!-- breaks the rule -->
</p>

Notes typed links, authorities and extra belong to records (V Records); in the text, they're errors.

Breaks text-note-type:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi maneant.<note type="links">See elsewhere.</note>
</p>

The import keeps a source note's words and codes until the project resolves them into relations; a parallel and an editorial note keep their words, and an editorial note its author. An error of the source marked by sic, or an addition by supplied, is an editorial note too (III.9.5 Illegible text and errors).

A reception note gives the later texts that took a passage over, as an edition lists them, often in a tier of its own under the sources: the collections that copied a canon, each by its book and chapter. Its words are the editor's; the codes of the later texts the corpus holds go in source, in the pointers' forms (IV.4 Sources and the pointers' forms). For this type, source names the later texts, not the passage's sources: the note's type says which way its codes point, where TEI reads source as the source of what the element holds. The codes of later texts the corpus doesn't hold yet are added when it does, and the words stay.

Whether a note is a reception note or a parallel follows the edition's form, not the dates: where the edition gives the later texts that took the passage, in a tier or a margin of their own, the note is a reception note; a "Cf." stays a parallel, whatever its date, as the note on the Council of Mainz of 847 above. The encoder records the edition's claim. One who has checked a later text and states that it took the passage over writes a relation of their own (VI Relations).

The Collectio Dionysio-Hadriana, in the edition of 1525, gives in its margins the chapters of Gratian's Decretum that took each canon over. The seventh of the Apostles' canons is Gratian's D. 88 c. 3:

<div type="chapter" n="7">
 <head type="number">Cap. VII.</head>
 <head type="rubric">Ut sacerdotes, et ministri altaris saecularibus curis abstineant.</head>
 <p>VII Episcopus , aut presbyter, aut diaconus nequaquam saeculares curas adsumat, sin aliter
   deiiciatur.<note type="reception"
   source="COLL.Grat.ed-SCTA_1.88.3">Gratian, Dist. 88. c. Episcopus aut
     sacerdos.</note>
 </p>
</div>

A passage that runs over several paragraphs, or whose ends other entries share, is marked by two anchors, and its reception note stands in the standOff (IV.8.2 Anchors). A made-up example, whose later texts the corpus holds in part:

<p>
 <anchor xml:id="taken-over-start"/>Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant.
</p>
<p>Si quis autem migrauerit, deponatur.<anchor xml:id="taken-over-end"/>
</p>
<listAnnotation>
 <annotation xml:id="later-collections"
  target="#range(taken-over-start,taken-over-end)">
  <note type="reception"
   source="COLL.Burch.ed-PL140_1.135">Burchard I, 135; Ivo, Decr. V, 234.</note>
 </annotation>
</listAnnotation>

The import keeps a reception note's words and codes as it keeps a source note's, and reads its codes the other way (VI.3 The levels' relations).

Element notechange

IV.6 Dates

A date in the text is marked by date, in the one form of dates that records and relations use too: the wording as the text gives it, and its bounds in attributes. It names the calendar of its words: a day the text reckons in the Julian calendar says so, and its bounds give the Gregorian day (VII Dates). The coded files mark none yet; the example is made up: V Idus Iunias is 9 June in the Julian calendar, 13 June in the Gregorian in 813.

<calendarDesc>
 <calendar xml:id="julian-mainz">
  <p>The Julian calendar.</p>
 </calendar>
</calendarDesc>
<p>… Data <date when="0813-06-13"
  calendar="#julian-mainz">V Idus Iunias</date> …</p>

IV.7 Languages

Words in another language than the text's are marked by foreign, the language in xml:lang; the text's own language is said once in the header. A made-up example:

<p>… quod Graeci <foreign xml:lang="grc">ὁμοούσιον</foreign> dicunt …</p>

IV.8 What no element fits

TEI has an element for most of what the layers mark. For the rest:

  • a kind of mark with no element of its own is a seg, typed by its layer, and its term, where the layer has terms, in ana, pointing to a category of a taxonomy declared in the header;
  • a record of a kind no name element fits, a diocese or a religious order, is an rs, its kind in type and its record in ref, in the forms of the names' records;
  • a span that crosses others or the structure, a quotation over several paragraphs, is split into fragments;
  • a point, or a span that can't be marked inline at all, is marked by anchors, which a stand-off annotation points to.

A segment's term, made up:

<taxonomy xml:id="formulae">
 <category xml:id="formula-of-the-law">
  <catDesc>A formula that cites the law</catDesc>
 </category>
</taxonomy>
<p>… <seg type="formula"
  ana="#formula-of-the-law">ut lex dicit</seg> …</p>

Breaks seg-term-declared:

<p>
 <seg type="formula"
  ana="#formula-of-authority">ut lex dicit</seg>
</p>

A record of another kind, made up:

<p>… per <rs type="diocese"
  ref="#new_diocese_Moguntia">dioecesim</rs> …</p>

Breaks record-pointer-form:

<p>Placuit ut episcopi per <rs type="diocese"
  ref="new_diocese_Samplia">dioecesim</rs>
<!-- breaks the rule --> maneant.</p>

IV.8.1 Fragments

A span that can't nest where it stands is split into fragments, each nesting in its paragraph or heading, joined by next and prev: each fragment has an xml:id, each but the last points to the next by next, and each but the first to the one before by prev. The Collectio 400 capitulorum quotes two passages over several paragraphs; they're fragments. The rules:

  • each fragment's next and prev point to an element of the same kind in the file, which points back (fragment-next, fragment-prev);
  • next points to an element later in the file: the fragments follow the text's order, so a chain has one first fragment and no loop (fragment-order);
  • only the first fragment carries the attributes that describe the whole span, a quotation's source, a segment's ana, a name's ref; a later one carries its xml:id, prev and next, and a segment's type, which the RelaxNG requires on every segment, with the first fragment's value (fragment-attributes). So two fragments can't disagree.

A quotation over two paragraphs, and a formula over two, made up:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="fragments-a"
   next="#fragments-b" source="C.NIC-0325_15">Placuit ut episcopi in
     ciuitatibus suis</quote>
 </p>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="fragments-b"
   prev="#fragments-a">maneant.</quote>
 </p>
</div>
<div type="chapter" n="2">
 <head type="number">II.</head>
 <p>Placuit ut <seg type="formula" xml:id="formula-a"
   next="#formula-b">si quis</seg>
 </p>
 <p>
  <seg type="formula" xml:id="formula-b"
   prev="#formula-a">episcopus</seg> migrauerit, deponatur.</p>
</div>

Breaks fragment-next:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="next-wrong-a"
   next="#next-wrong-c">Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis</quote>
 </p>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="next-wrong-b">maneant.</quote>
 </p>
</div>

Breaks fragment-prev:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="prev-wrong-a">Placuit ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis</quote>
 </p>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="prev-wrong-b"
   prev="#prev-wrong-a">maneant.</quote>
 </p>
</div>

Breaks fragment-order:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="order-wrong-b"
   prev="#order-wrong-a">maneant.</quote>
 </p>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="order-wrong-a"
   next="#order-wrong-b" source="C.NIC-0325_15">Placuit ut episcopi in
     ciuitatibus suis</quote>
 </p>
</div>

Breaks fragment-attributes:

<div type="chapter" n="1">
 <head type="number">I.</head>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="attributes-wrong-a"
   next="#attributes-wrong-b" source="C.NIC-0325_15">Placuit ut episcopi
     in ciuitatibus suis</quote>
 </p>
 <p>
  <quote xml:id="attributes-wrong-b"
   prev="#attributes-wrong-a" source="C.NIC-0325_15">maneant.</quote>
 </p>
</div>

The import makes one annotation of the fragments, over the whole span.

IV.8.2 Anchors

An anchor marks a point. A stand-off annotation, in the standOff's listAnnotation, points to one anchor, or to the range between two by TEI's range(). It's the form for what can't be marked inline at all; an export uses it for annotations that cross stronger ones (X Exports). A made-up example:

<p>Placuit <anchor xml:id="span-start"/>ut episcopi in ciuitatibus suis maneant<anchor xml:id="span-end"/>.</p>
<listAnnotation>
 <annotation xml:id="annotation-1"
  target="#range(span-start,span-end)">
  <note type="editorial">A remark on the whole clause.</note>
 </annotation>
</listAnnotation>
